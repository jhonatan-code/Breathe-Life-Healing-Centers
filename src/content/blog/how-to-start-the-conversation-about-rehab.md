## Key takeaways

- **Preparation matters:** understand the Stages of Change so you can match your approach to your loved one's readiness.
- **Use CRAFT:** the Community Reinforcement and Family Training method rewards positive behavior instead of punishing negative behavior.
- **Environment matters:** choose a private, calm setting and avoid intoxication or high-stress moments.
- **Collaborate, don't confront:** frame treatment as a partnership, with real choices about level of care.
- **The goal:** move from conflict to a supportive partnership that empowers your loved one to choose recovery.

The person you're trying to reach isn't operating from the same emotional framework you are. Substance use changes the brain's threat response in ways that can turn concern into something that feels like attack, and love into something that feels like control. The pattern is familiar: someone gathers their courage, rehearses what to say, and within minutes the conversation derails, voices rise, and the person retreats further into isolation, now armed with fresh justification.

What actually happened is usually shame. Shame is one of addiction's most effective defenses. Someone who feels exposed or judged doesn't hear "I'm worried about you," they hear "you're broken and failing." Bad timing makes it worse: confronting someone while intoxicated, in withdrawal, or emotionally volatile all but guarantees failure, and language like "you're destroying your life" activates defensiveness instead of openness, however honest it feels in the moment.

None of this makes a good outcome impossible. It just means the conversation needs real structure.

## Understand where they are: the Stages of Change

The Stages of Change model describes how people move through precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, and maintenance when facing a difficult behavior change. Someone in precontemplation isn't considering change at all, and may get defensive at even a gentle question; direct advice or pressure tends to backfire here. Someone in contemplation is ambivalent, maybe voicing something like "I'm tired of feeling this way," which opens a real window for respectful questions and listening. Nearly 40 percent of people who need help are still in precontemplation, so matching your approach to where someone actually is, rather than where you wish they were, changes the odds significantly. Research has found that moving someone from precontemplation to contemplation within the first month of conversation roughly doubles their chances of taking action within six months.

## Language that opens the door, and language that closes it

Word choice can make or break the moment. Stigmatizing terms send an unmistakable signal of blame, and the National Institute on Drug Abuse has found that stigmatizing language actually lowers the chance someone will seek help at all. Person-first language keeps the door open instead.

| Avoid | Use instead | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Addict / junkie | Person living with a substance use disorder | Doesn't reduce a person to their illness |
| Alcoholic | Person experiencing alcohol challenges | Avoids implying permanence over identity |
| Clean / dirty | In recovery / experiencing symptoms | "Dirty" reinforces shame and secrecy |

## Step 1: Get the timing and setting right

Timing is everything. Well-intentioned conversations routinely fall apart because someone brought up treatment right after an argument, at a family gathering, or while the person was actively intoxicated. The best conversations happen during calm, sober moments, early morning before the day builds stress, or a quiet, relaxed evening, never as an ambush at a party or right after use.

Skip the conversation entirely if your loved one is intoxicated (they're unlikely to process it), in crisis (high stress produces defensiveness, not openness), or emotionally withdrawn (they can't engage in a vulnerable dialogue from that place). A private setting, a quiet living room or a walk, beats a crowded intervention almost every time, and giving the conversation room to breathe, rather than rushing it, signals that you care about them and not just the outcome.

## Step 2: Use the CRAFT approach

Community Reinforcement and Family Training (CRAFT) replaces confrontation with connection. At its core, CRAFT means reinforcing positive behavior and allowing natural consequences for negative behavior, rather than lecturing about what's wrong. If your loved one comes home sober and engaged one evening, that's your opening: "I really enjoyed talking with you tonight. It means a lot to see you present." When they're using, step back without punishment, not ignoring the problem, but declining to enable or absorb its chaos: "I can't help with that right now, but I'd love to talk tomorrow when you're feeling better" maintains the boundary while keeping the door open.

This shift matters more than it might sound: families using CRAFT see their loved one enter treatment at a rate of roughly 65 to 75 percent, compared to 20 to 30 percent with older, more confrontational approaches.

Open-ended, curiosity-driven questions, the heart of motivational interviewing, do more than lecturing ever will: "What are some things you wish were different in your life right now?" or "How do you feel about where things are headed?" invite reflection instead of forcing anyone into a corner.

## Step 3: Set boundaries, not ultimatums

A boundary and an ultimatum sound similar but work differently. An ultimatum says "do this or else." A boundary says "I can't continue to enable these behaviors, and here's what that means for me." Instead of "get help by Friday or I'm leaving," try "I love you, but I can no longer cover for you at work or lend you money when you're using. I'm here to support your recovery, not your addiction."

Enabling isn't about loving too much, it's unintentionally smoothing over the consequences of someone's substance use.

| Enabling | Supportive instead |
|---|---|
| Lying to cover missed work | Encouraging honest communication |
| Paying fines or debts | Offering emotional support, not money |
| Making excuses to others | Setting clear, loving boundaries |
| Ignoring risky behavior | Acknowledging concerns directly |

Supporting someone through this is genuinely exhausting, and burnout among family members is common. Research links family members who prioritize their own health to better outcomes for their loved one too, so this isn't a detour from the work, it's part of it.

## Step 4: Present treatment as collaboration

The most common mistake is presenting treatment like a punishment. Frame it instead as something you're doing together. Instead of "if you don't get help, you're going to lose everything," try "you've been fighting this alone for too long. What if we found a place where you had a whole team supporting you?" Offering real choices, rather than demands, gives someone a sense of agency: "there are a few different ways people find help, would you like to look at some together?"

Understanding the actual levels of care available helps reduce fear and confusion:

| Level of care | What it includes | Who it's for |
|---|---|---|
| Medically supervised detox | 24/7 medical and psychological care, withdrawal management | Those needing safe withdrawal, or facing health risks like seizures |
| Residential (primary) | Live-in, structured therapy, trauma-informed, group and individual work | Complex cases, dual diagnosis, those needing daily structure |
| Partial hospitalization (PHP) | Daytime therapy and groups, home or recovery housing at night | Those stepping down, or with a supportive home environment |
| Enhanced sober living / outpatient | Flexible, strengths-based support, therapy several times a week | Maintenance, returning to work or school, ongoing support |

People are far more open to treatment when their specific needs feel respected. Someone who identifies as LGBTQIA+, for example, may worry about whether treatment will feel safe for them. At Breathe, specialty tracks include trauma-informed care that addresses emotional safety from day one, identity-affirming LGBTQIA+ programming, a curriculum built specifically for methamphetamine recovery, and eating-disorder treatment that combines trauma care with nutritional stabilization. It's worth naming these options directly. Treatment can genuinely be tailored to trauma history, gender identity, and co-occurring mental health needs.

## What doesn't work

The intervention-style ambush, a room full of people and an ultimatum, tends to create defensiveness rather than openness. So does threatening a consequence you won't actually enforce: saying "I'll stop supporting you" while continuing to enable teaches someone that your words don't match your actions.

The idea that someone has to "hit rock bottom" before they're ready is one of the most stubborn and least accurate myths in this space. Substance use disorders are progressive, and waiting for things to get worse only raises the risk and delays recovery. Just because someone hasn't lost everything doesn't mean they aren't ready for help, and early, compassionate conversations can prevent real harm down the line. Shame-based language ("you're ruining your life") isolates rather than motivates. Curiosity and compassion, "I care about you and want to understand what you're feeling," open dialogue in a way threats never do.
