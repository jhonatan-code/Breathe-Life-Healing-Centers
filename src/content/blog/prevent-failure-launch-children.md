"Failure to launch" is a term that comes from a 2006 comedy about a man who refuses to move out of his parents' house well into his thirties. Back then it was played for laughs. Today it describes something real: a growing number of young adults who struggle to move out and become fully independent, and a growing number of worried parents watching it happen. Surveys have found that a substantial share of young adults between 18 and 34, even those with jobs, are living back at home with their parents. Almost no one is laughing anymore.

## We want our kids to succeed. That's part of the problem.

Every parent wants their child to succeed, badly. But that intense desire, and the parenting style it tends to produce, may be part of what's driving the failure-to-launch pattern. Something in how many of us were raised, and how many of us are now raising our own kids, isn't working for anyone.

The trend was first observed seriously in people born between 1980 and 2000. Around that time, growing fear about the job market and the future shaped how an entire generation of parents raised and educated their children: intensive college preparation, competitive sports and activities, lessons for everything, less unstructured play. The underlying belief was that anything less than exceptional meant failure, so parents began demanding higher and higher achievement.

For kids raised that way, the message often wasn't "we believe in you," it was "average is unacceptable." Constant messaging about a shrinking job market and a rising cost of living taught a generation to feel afraid and insecure about their own prospects, even when they were doing well by any reasonable measure. Fear, self-doubt, and a sense of inadequacy took root early, and for many people, they never fully went away.

## What's actually behind it

A lack of confidence in one's own ability to handle the world is at the center of failure to launch. Young adults stuck at home generally aren't lazy or trying to avoid responsibility. Many are genuinely afraid of not being able to make it on their own, a fear shaped as much by parenting as by the wider economy. Packing a child's schedule with enrichment activities, meant lovingly, can send the opposite message: that they aren't equipped to handle what's coming without constant structure and support. Pressure aimed at preventing mediocrity can end up teaching a child to fear being average more than almost anything else.

Boredom gets treated like something to eliminate, but it isn't the enemy. Unstructured, unscheduled time is often what pushes a child to think creatively, get curious, and develop their own interests, exactly the skills that make independence possible later.

## What helps

It's best to start young, but it's never too late to help an older child build these skills.

### Encourage them to set their own goals

Working toward goals they've chosen for themselves, not ones handed to them, is what prepares a young person for adult life, where no one else will be setting the agenda. If your child wants to work toward something, even something that seems small or unimportant to you, let them pursue it and feel what it's like to achieve it on their own.

### Be a little more hands-off

Keep scheduled activities to one or two rather than filling every hour. Free time gives a child room to explore, experiment, and learn to entertain themselves, skills adulthood requires and a packed schedule doesn't teach. Adulthood doesn't come with a planner. It's a blank slate a person has to learn to fill on their own.

### Don't overreact to mistakes

A disappointing grade or a missed goal is a normal part of growing up, not a catastrophe. Responding to mistakes with support rather than punishment teaches a child that it's safe to fail and try again. Overreacting teaches the opposite, and an intense fear of failure is exactly what shows up again and again in kids who struggle to launch. Ask yourself honestly: have you succeeded at everything you've ever tried? What did the failures teach you? A single bad grade is rarely the real problem. The fear an overreaction creates is what follows a child into adulthood.

### Give unconditional love

Make sure your child knows you love them for who they are, not only for what they achieve. Being proud of their accomplishments is natural, but if love feels conditional on success, it teaches insecurity instead of confidence. A child who grows up feeling unconditionally loved tends to develop a stronger sense of identity and healthier relationships later in life. A child who feels love is conditional often carries fear and insecurity into those same relationships.
